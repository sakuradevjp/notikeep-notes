1. Background: why the built-in notification history isn't enough

Android 11 added an opt-in Notification history screen buried under Settings → Notifications. It works, but it's intentionally minimal: it keeps the last 24 hours, has no search, no filter, no images, and no way to mark anything as important. Once a notification ages out, it's gone.

For the everyday case — "I dismissed a delivery code by accident" — that 24-hour window is usually enough. For anything beyond that it isn't. Anyone who has tried to go back to a LINE message from last week, look up which app sent a particular receipt, or recover an SMS one-time-code three days after the fact has hit the same wall: the notification was real, the system saw it, and now it's unreachable.

Third-party notification loggers exist to fill that gap. Most of them solve the storage problem and stop there: they listen for onNotificationPosted, dump the title and text into SQLite, and present a flat reverse-chronological list. Open the list and 80% of what you see is junk you never wanted to look at — "Backing up to Drive…", "USB debugging connected", "X is sending your post…", a foreground-service notification from a screen recorder, an importance-MIN ping from Play Protect.

The signal-to-noise ratio is the actual problem with this category, and it's the one we set out to solve.

2. What NotiKeep actually does

NotiKeep is an Android NotificationListenerService-based archive. Notifications come in, go through a filter that drops the obvious noise, and the survivors are written to a local Room database with their images attached. From there you can search, bookmark, mute by title, block by app, and tap a saved notification to jump back to the original content where the source app supports it.

A few details worth calling out, because they're the parts that took the most work and aren't obvious from a screenshot:

  • Image preservation. When a notification carries a largeIcon or a BigPictureStyle attachment, NotiKeep extracts and stores it as WebP. Saved photos from messaging apps, news thumbnails, and similar imagery survive as part of the archive. The system itself drops these the moment the user dismisses the notification.
  • Per-app bell control. Two distinct modes per app: keep the OS notification visible (default behavior, NotiKeep just records), or silently archive (NotiKeep records and immediately calls cancelNotification() so the tray stays clean). CATEGORY_CALL and CATEGORY_ALARM are protected — the silent-archive mode never applies to those, regardless of setting.
  • Mute by title and block by app. Independent from the silent filter. Mute-by-title is for apps that legitimately ship important notifications mixed with noise — you keep the channel open but suppress specific recurring titles.
  • Encrypted backup with images. A single ZIP that contains the database, shared preferences, and the image directory. Restoring on a new device rebuilds the archive byte-for-byte, including images, mute lists, blocked apps, and bookmarks.
  • Bookmark for keepers. Notifications past the retention window are pruned automatically. Bookmarked ones are exempt and stick around until you remove them — the equivalent of starring an email for permanent reference.

The retention window is the policy lever. Free tier keeps 30 days, which covers the "I dismissed something earlier today" case comfortably and a couple of weekends past that. Pro lets you set anything from 30 days to unlimited, with bookmarks always exempt either way.

3. The Image Timeline — and why nothing else in this category does it

This is the feature we'd point at first if asked what makes NotiKeep different from every other notification logger on Google Play.

Plenty of notifications carry images. Instagram and Threads ship the post thumbnail. News apps attach the article hero. Messaging apps embed the photo someone just sent you. WhatsApp Web pairing, profile-picture-changed alerts, restaurant booking confirmations with the venue photo, package-delivered confirmations with the photo of the box on the doorstep — all of these arrive as BigPictureStyle notifications with a real bitmap attached. And then, twenty minutes later, you swipe the notification away and the image is gone.

The Image Timeline is the part of NotiKeep that exists to keep those images. Not their URLs (which often expire), not the surrounding text (which the archive already has), but the actual pixels — extracted on receipt, re-encoded as WebP, and stored on the device. The dedicated view then surfaces them as a date-grouped grid you can scroll through.

Why other apps don't do this

We looked at a fair number of notification loggers before building this, and the standard pattern is to either (a) ignore images entirely and just save text, or (b) save the icon URL string and call it a day. We don't think anyone is doing actual bitmap extraction at receive-time and presenting it as a separate timeline. There are reasons for that:

  • Notification image bitmaps are bound to the notification's lifecycle. When the user dismisses a notification (or the source app cancels it), the OS releases the image. If you want to keep it, you have to read the bitmap out of the live notification and copy it before the dismissal happens. That requires doing real work in onNotificationPosted rather than just serializing strings.
  • Storage isn't free. A typical attached image is 100KB–1MB as PNG. Across a few months of notifications you can easily hit several hundred megabytes if you don't compress. NotiKeep re-encodes to WebP at a sensible quality target, which gets typical attachments down to 20–80KB while keeping them visually intact.
  • Privacy considerations. Once you commit to keeping notification images on disk, you're also signing up for "make sure they don't leak". Backups need to bundle them as part of the encrypted ZIP. The image directory can't sit in shared external storage where another app could read it. Restoring on a new device has to remap absolute paths to the new install. There's a non-trivial amount of plumbing involved that most loggers don't sign up for.
  • It's a niche use case. Most users don't think "I want to flip through old notification images". Until they do, and realize there's no way to get back to the photo a friend sent two weeks ago that they never saved properly. The Image Timeline isn't an everyday feature; it's a feature you remember exists exactly when you need it.

What it's actually useful for

Use cases we've heard from users (and ones we use it for ourselves):

  • Recovering a photo a friend sent in a messaging app you don't otherwise back up
  • Looking up the venue/dish/product image from a confirmation notification when you forgot to bookmark the order itself
  • Browsing news app thumbnails by date — surprisingly useful for "what was the headline last Tuesday?"
  • Catching screenshot images shared via apps that delete content after viewing
  • Visual scanning for that specific notification when you can't quite remember the sender's name but you remember what the photo looked like
The Image Timeline isn't a feature most users will open every day. It's the feature that makes the difference the one time it matters — and it's the one part of NotiKeep we couldn't find in any competing notification logger we tested.

The Image Timeline is part of the Pro tier (along with unlimited retention and full backup). The reasoning is straightforward: the work that goes into preserving images — bitmap extraction, WebP encoding, secure storage, backup integration — is meaningfully more than text-only logging, and the storage footprint over time is real. Free tier still preserves the images attached to individual notifications and shows them in the detail view; the timeline view itself is what unlocks with Pro.

4. The "noise" problem — and how the filter is built

A typical Android user receives somewhere between 50 and 200 notifications a day. Of those, a substantial fraction is structurally not worth saving:

  • Progress notifications — file uploads, downloads, syncs, "Posting…" indicators. They update many times per second while in flight, then disappear when complete.
  • Foreground-service notifications — the persistent "App is running in the background" pings that Android requires from any long-running service.
  • Media playback controls — CATEGORY_TRANSPORT notifications from music apps. Useful while playing, meaningless in an archive.
  • System notifications — USB debugging, Play Protect scans, OS update reminders, "Connected as a media device".
  • IMPORTANCE_MIN pings — the lowest-importance channel, used for things the system itself considers ignorable.

A naive logger captures all of these. The user opens the archive, sees 200 entries, and 180 of them are progress bars and foreground services. The signal — the actual messages, codes, alerts — is buried.

Notification archives only become useful when the obvious noise is filtered out. The threshold isn't whether a notification was technically posted — it's whether a human reading the archive a week later would consider it worth saving.

NotiKeep's filter runs on every onNotificationPosted and applies a sequence of checks. Each check has a corresponding side effect — whether to drop silently, drop and clear the tray, or save:

  • CATEGORY_TRANSPORT, CATEGORY_PROGRESS, CATEGORY_SERVICE → drop and clear
  • FLAG_FOREGROUND_SERVICE → drop and clear
  • Has progress extra and category is null/STATUS → drop and clear (catches "X is uploading…" style)
  • CATEGORY_SYSTEM + FLAG_ONGOING → drop and clear
  • FLAG_ONGOING + non-important category → drop and clear
  • Empty title and text → drop
  • IMPORTANCE_MIN → drop and clear
  • Same package + same title + same text within a few seconds → drop as duplicate
  • Otherwise → save

The "drop and clear" cases also call cancelNotification(), which removes the notification from the tray. This is the bell-off behavior: NotiKeep recognizes the notification as something the user shouldn't have to deal with at all, archives nothing, and silently sweeps it out of the way.

A few categories are explicitly protected from the ongoing-notification filter even when they carry FLAG_ONGOING: MESSAGE, SOCIAL, EVENT, REMINDER, ALARM, and CALL. An ongoing message thread or alarm is real signal — the persistence flag just reflects how the source app structures its UI.

One filter case that took a few iterations: progress + email category. Gmail labels new mail with CATEGORY_EMAIL and occasionally also sets a progress extra during sync. Initially the filter dropped these as progress notifications. The fix was to require both a progress extra and a null/STATUS category before treating it as a progress drop — if a real category is present, the progress extra is incidental and the notification gets saved.

What still gets through: we drop the obvious noise structurally, but app developers occasionally use CATEGORY_MESSAGE for non-message things (marketing pushes, "you've earned X points") and there's no automatic way to detect that. The mute-by-title list is the manual escape hatch — add a recurring title once and it stops appearing in the archive.

5. Deep-linking back to content — the 1,107-dump research

An archive that just holds text is useful. An archive that lets you tap an entry and jump back to the original post in the source app is more useful, and it's what most loggers don't do.

The standard approach is to fire the notification's contentIntent (a PendingIntent the source app provided). This works when the source app is well-behaved, but it has known failure modes: cached PendingIntents that resolve to stale content, Android 14+ background-activity-launch restrictions, cold-start cases where the source process has died and the deep-link metadata went with it. For some apps — Instagram is currently the worst offender — a saved PendingIntent fired even seconds later silently lands on the home feed instead of the original post.

The alternative is to extract a content ID from the notification's extras bundle and construct an explicit vnd.app://-style URI. That's faster, more reliable when it works, and survives across source-app process death. The catch: every app structures its extras differently, and most of the keys aren't documented anywhere.

In plain terms: we wanted to tap an old YouTube notification and go straight to that exact video, even if YouTube had been killed and restarted in between. To do that, we had to know which extras key carries the video ID — and the only way to find out was to look at real notifications.

Over several months we collected and inspected 1,107 notification-extras dumps across 39 apps. The dump for each notification is the entire Bundle — every key the source app put in, plus every standard Android key. The summary of what came out of that:

  • YouTube (310 dumps) — the chime.slot_key extra carries the 11-character video ID 66% of the time, and the vnd.youtube://{id} URI scheme is public. Implemented and reliable.
  • X / Twitter (150 dumps) — zero custom extras keys. No tweet ID anywhere in the bundle. Fall back to PendingIntent.
  • Gmail (121 dumps) — only the account name. No message ID, no thread ID. Fall back to PendingIntent.
  • Instagram (46 dumps) — extras carry useful IDs, but Instagram's own deep-link schemes don't accept them in a useful way. We tested the obvious URIs on a real device and got either browser-disambiguation dialogs or wrong content. Decided not to ship Instagram deep-linking; it would degrade compared to PendingIntent.
  • Threads (56 dumps) — similar story to Instagram, plus most notifications are status pings without IDs at all.
  • LINE (11 dumps) — chat ID is reliably present. line://msg/chat/{id} is plausible but needs more on-device verification.
  • Mattermost, Google Messages, others — extras carry IDs but the deep-link schemes either aren't public or require state the notification doesn't carry (team slug, account context, etc.).
  • 30+ other apps — no useful custom keys. Fall back to PendingIntent.

The honest takeaway from that work is that most apps do not give you what you'd need to deep-link reliably. The marketing line "tap any notification to jump back to the original" is mostly aspirational across the Android app ecosystem. Where the data is there — YouTube being the cleanest case — we use it. Where it isn't, we fall back to the source app's PendingIntent and accept the failure modes that come with it.

The full per-app breakdown is checked into the project's internal docs and is the basis for what NotiKeep does and doesn't try to deep-link. As source apps change their notification structure across releases, that table changes too, and the implementation needs to track it.

Why we don't go further: there are non-public APIs that would expose more. We don't use them. They break across OS updates, fail Play Store policy review on the wrong day, and aren't worth the maintenance cost on an app that's supposed to be about the archive, not the deep-link.

6. Permissions and privacy

The one permission this category cannot avoid is Notification Access (BIND_NOTIFICATION_LISTENER_SERVICE). It's the API that makes the whole thing possible. The tradeoff is real: granting it means the app sees every notification the system delivers. There is no notification logger of any kind on Android without this grant.

The setup flow puts this front and center — the onboarding has a dedicated step that opens the system settings page directly, with an explanation of what the permission unlocks and what it does not. Once granted, the listener runs in NotiKeep's own process; nothing is forwarded anywhere off-device.

In plain terms: Notification Access is a big permission. It exists because Android can't allow a less-powerful version of it for this use case. We try to be transparent about that during onboarding rather than burying it.

Beyond that, the runtime permission set is minimal:

  • No location, contacts, SMS, microphone, camera, calendar, or storage permissions. None of these are requested at any point.
  • INTERNET is declared — required by the Google Play Billing library for the Pro subscription. The app makes no other network calls. No analytics SDK, no crash-reporting SDK, no remote-config SDK is bundled. Removing the IAP code would let us drop the network permission entirely; we chose not to make that trade because subscription-based monetization keeps the product ad-free.
  • All notification data — text, images, mute lists, bookmarks, settings — is stored in the app's private data directory. Nothing is uploaded.
  • Backups are user-initiated and user-located. The export goes to a path the user picks via the Storage Access Framework, and the restore reads from a path the user picks. No automatic cloud upload.

For comparison, most free notification loggers on Google Play bundle an ad SDK (AdMob, Unity, IronSource, AppLovin), which brings the INTERNET permission plus the tracker bundle that comes with it. Some also request READ_PHONE_STATE and ACCESS_NETWORK_STATE for ad-network targeting. NotiKeep's permission list, including the unavoidable Notification Access, fits on one screen.

7. How we compare to other notification loggers

The category breaks down into three rough buckets:

  • OS-built-in — Android 11+ Notification History. Always available, free, no extra permission grant needed (it's built into the system). 24-hour retention, no search, no filter, no images, no archiving.
  • Third-party loggers — Notisave, "Notification History Log", "Notification Saver", and a long tail of similar apps. Most are ad-supported, capture-everything style, with minimal filtering.
  • Notification mirroring tools — KDE Connect, Pushbullet, AirDroid. Different category: their job is to forward notifications to another device in real time, not to maintain a searchable archive. Useful for what they do, but not substitutes for an archive.

8. Pricing

The free tier is meant to be useful, not a demo. Core archiving, the noise filter, search across the last 30 days, image preservation, and 5 bookmark slots all work without paying.

Pro unlocks the rest:

  • Unlimited retention (configurable from 30 days up to no expiry)
  • Unlimited bookmarks, mute-by-title entries, and blocked apps
  • Full-text search across the entire archive
  • Backup and restore with images
  • Image timeline (a separate view that surfaces only notifications with attached images)
  • YouTube browser-mode launch (opt-in alternative for the YouTube deep-link)
  • Three custom theme slots

Pricing options are $0.99 / month, $9.99 / year, or $19.99 one-time. Prices are USD list and subject to Google's regional pricing.

Every install starts with a 30-day free trial of the full Pro feature set. The trial is implemented in-app rather than through Google Play's trial system — at the end of 30 days the app reverts to Free with no charge and no auto-conversion. To stay on Pro, the user has to actively pick a plan from the upgrade screen. We made that an explicit choice rather than the default because nobody likes accidentally getting billed at the end of a trial.

Monetization being a paid subscription rather than ads is also why the app ships without an ad SDK and without the network-permission footprint that comes with one. That's a real trade we're making — fewer downloads in absolute terms, but a clean permission list and no third-party code paths in the listener.

9. Summary — who this is for

Who is this for? Honest take

We don't think this app is for everyone. If the built-in 24-hour notification history covers your use case, the simplest answer is to just use that. The audience NotiKeep is built for is the user who has hit the wall on the built-in feature more than once — needed an SMS code from three days ago, lost a delivery notification to an accidental swipe, wanted to find that one X DM with the link in it — and decided that having a real archive on the device is worth a permission grant and roughly $1 a month. For that audience we tried to make the cleanest version of the product the category will allow: filter, images, search, backup, no ads, no trackers, transparent permission list.

Pros

  • Image Timeline — the only product in this category that extracts notification image bitmaps and presents them as a browsable grid
  • Filter actually drops the obvious noise — progress, foreground services, system pings, low-importance pings
  • Filtering rules and deep-link mappings grounded in real notification analysis (1,107 dumps, 39 apps)
  • Encrypted ZIP backup including images, mute lists, and bookmarks — full-state device migration
  • No ads, no analytics SDKs, no trackers on any tier
  • Permission list fits on one screen — only Notification Access and INTERNET (for billing)
  • 30-day in-app trial, no auto-conversion at the end
  • YouTube deep-link works reliably; LINE is in progress
  • Localized into 12 languages

Cons

  • Notification Access is unavoidable for any app in this category, and it's a meaningful grant. We try to be transparent about it during onboarding, but the trade is real
  • End-to-end-encrypted apps (Signal, encrypted iMessage previews) only surface "New message" placeholders to the OS — there's no content to archive, regardless of logger
  • If the user blocks an app's notifications at the OS level, NotiKeep can't see them. By design — we don't try to bypass user-side OS settings
  • Deep-linking only works where the source app exposes content IDs in extras. YouTube yes, X/Gmail/Instagram effectively no — we documented this honestly rather than pretending universal deep-link support
  • Solo dev. If you hit an edge case on a non-Pixel ROM or an obscure app, expect investigation before a fix
  • Initial onboarding has a few steps (Notification Access grant, optional battery-optimization exemption, language pick). Unavoidable for a listener-based app, but it's friction
